Fall Protection

The 5 Most Common Fall Protection Mistakes on Today's Jobsites

Falls remain the leading cause of death in construction. From improper anchor points to missing rescue plans, here are the five mistakes we see most often — and exactly how to fix them before your next inspection.

MS
Marcus Steele Lead Safety Instructor
Feb 14, 2026 6 min read

If there's one hazard that shows up in nearly every fatal construction accident report, it's falls. According to OSHA, falls account for roughly one in three construction deaths — a number that has stayed stubbornly high even as other categories have improved.

The frustrating part? Most falls are preventable. In our years training crews across the Northwest, we see the same handful of mistakes repeated over and over. Here are the five we encounter most — and what to do about each.

1. Skipping the Rescue Plan

A personal fall arrest system isn't designed to hold a worker indefinitely. After a fall, suspension trauma can set in within 15 to 20 minutes. Yet many sites install the harness and the lanyard without ever writing a rescue plan.

Every fall protection plan needs a rescue plan attached to it. If you can't answer "who gets them down, and with what?" — you don't have a complete plan.

The fix is straightforward: identify the rescue method before work begins, train at least two people on the crew to execute it, and keep the necessary equipment on site.

2. Improper Anchor Points

Not all anchor points are created equal. OSHA requires anchors capable of supporting 5,000 pounds per worker attached, or designed by a qualified person with a safety factor of at least two.

Quick Rule of Thumb

If you can't find the rated capacity stamped or documented, don't anchor to it. When in doubt, engineer it out.

3. Using the Wrong Equipment for the Task

A self-retracting lifeline isn't the same as a shock-absorbing lanyard, and a positioning harness isn't a fall arrest harness. Mixing them up — or using whatever's in the truck — is a recipe for disaster.

Common equipment mismatches we see:

  • Shock-absorbing lanyards used where free-fall distance exceeds limits
  • Positioning belts used as fall arrest systems
  • Self-retracting lifelines mounted at the wrong angle
  • Carabiners loaded on the gate rather than the spine

The fix starts with a proper hazard assessment. Match the equipment to the task — not the other way around.

4. Ignoring Fall Clearance Calculations

This is the silent killer. A worker can be fully tied off, properly anchored, and still hit the ground — because nobody calculated the total fall distance.

Total fall distance includes: free fall, deceleration distance, the worker's height, and a safety margin. Add a swing fall hazard and the numbers get worse fast.

5. Treating Training as a One-Time Event

Fall protection isn't a checkbox. Equipment changes. Site conditions change. Crews change. Yet too many companies treat the initial training as the finish line rather than the starting point.

The most effective programs we've seen build short, focused refreshers into the regular rhythm of the job: a 15-minute toolbox talk, a quarterly hands-on session, an annual full refresher.

The Bottom Line

Fall protection failures rarely come down to a single mistake. They're almost always a stack: incomplete planning, wrong equipment, missed calculations, and stale training, all coming together on the wrong day.

The good news is that each layer of the stack is fixable. Start with a rescue plan. Verify your anchors. Match your equipment. Run the numbers. Keep the training fresh.
